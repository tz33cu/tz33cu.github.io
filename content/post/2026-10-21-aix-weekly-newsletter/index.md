Many debates about AI turn into contests: can AI grade, tutor, or advise as well as a person? This issue starts from a different premise. AI governance is not about proving whether humans or AI are better at something. It is about how we choose to use technology in our lives.

<!--more-->

Capability comparisons tell us what is *possible*. They cannot tell us what we *want*. This month, a faculty council, a student senate, and a citizens' assembly each argued, in different words, for the same thing: a say in how AI is used where they live and work. The research this issue gathers points the same way. How people want AI used depends on the stakes and the setting, and sometimes "the AI is capable" is a convenient story we tell ourselves.

## TL;DR {#tldr}

Read side by side, this month's campus news shows a shift in what people are arguing about. The argument used to be about capability: is the tool good enough? Now it is about standing: who gets to decide how the tool is used here?

- **Faculty and students are asking "who decides?"** At Duke, faculty objected to an institution-wide decision to give students Copilot and ChatGPT and proposed a resolution giving them a seat at the table for enterprise AI decisions ([Duke Chronicle, Oct 2](https://dukechronicle.com/article/duke-university-arts-and-sciences-council-ai-takes-center-stage-with-proposed-resolution-on-faculty-governance-bluebot-20261002)). At Eastern Illinois, students unanimously asked their administration to regulate how *faculty* use AI. The president replied that curriculum belongs to the disciplines, so answers will vary by department ([Daily Eastern News, Oct 11](https://www.dailyeasternnews.com/2026/10/11/student-senate-asks-university-to-regulate-use-of-ai-by-faculty/)).
- **The public wants a voice too.** An Edinburgh assembly of 99 people from government, the NHS, industry, academia, and the public recommended a standing UK citizens' assembly on AI and society ([University of Edinburgh, June 10](https://www.sps.ed.ac.uk/news-events/news/citizens-assembly-public-voice-ai-report)).
- **Preferences depend on context and stakes.** Students favor human judgment for subjective work but accept AI feedback for lower-stakes tasks ([*Behavioral Sciences*, 2026](https://avesis.tedu.edu.tr/yayin/cda371d2-4fda-4cb1-a060-3ce3586e2bb1/when-do-undergraduate-students-prefer-ai-insights-into-ai-scoring-and-feedback)). In a randomized field study, AI drafts increased how often TAs gave feedback while leaving the TAs fully in control ([Mahinpei et al., June 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03095)).
- **"The AI can do it" can be a convenient belief.** In experiments with 5,639 participants, people delegated real moral decisions to AI *more* readily than to another person. Part of the reason was a self-serving shift in what they believed about AI's capability ([Hüholt & Szech, *European Economic Review*, 2026](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000190253)).

None of these groups argued that AI is incapable. They argued that the people affected should help choose, and the research suggests why that matters: when the stakes are moral, "the AI is capable" can be a way to avoid responsibility.

## Research Highlights

*Claude's note on the evidence: these are single studies with modest samples, except the moral-delegation experiments. One is a preprint. Read them as evidence about what people prefer and how they behave, not as verdicts on AI's capability.*

**1. Delegating moral decisions to AI** — *Hüholt & Szech, "Trusting machines with morality: Delegating moral decisions to AI," [European Economic Review 184 (2026), 105255](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000190253); online January 2026.*

*Evidence: two incentivized experiments, 5,639 participants total.*

Earlier survey research suggested people distrust AI in moral contexts and prefer human decision-makers. But when participants faced a real moral decision with consequences, they handed it off significantly *more* often when the delegate was an AI than when it was another person. The authors find that responsibility-shifting, a known motive for delegating to people, extends to AI. They also find it is helped along by people adjusting their beliefs about AI's capability in a self-serving way. The more ambiguous the AI's capability, the easier it is to justify letting it decide.

> *Editor's note:* This is the strongest reason I know not to let "is AI capable of X?" stand in for the governance question. Capability claims are ambiguous enough to be bent toward whatever we already wanted to do. Asking "do we want AI to decide this, and who is accountable if it does?" is harder to fudge. — TZ

**2. When do students prefer AI scoring and feedback?** — *Yildirim Erbasli, Dibek, Thomas & Lesoway, [Behavioral Sciences 16(7), 2026](https://avesis.tedu.edu.tr/yayin/cda371d2-4fda-4cb1-a060-3ce3586e2bb1/when-do-undergraduate-students-prefer-ai-insights-into-ai-scoring-and-feedback) (DOI 10.3390/bs16071196).*

*Evidence: survey and scenario study, 93 undergraduates.*

Students liked structured, moderately detailed AI feedback on grammar and organization, but generally preferred human evaluation for subjective tasks. They saw AI as useful in lower-stakes settings and doubted it could judge more complex writing. They favored hybrid models in which AI supports human judgment, with transparency and human review. A related Canadian study (159 students) found that students often could not tell whether a person or ChatGPT had written their feedback, yet learning the source lowered their confidence in AI *scoring* ([Sciforum, 2026](https://sciforum.net/paper/30087)).

> *Editor's note:* If students can't tell the feedback apart but still want a person to own the grade, that preference is not about quality. It is about who should be accountable for a judgment of their work. That is a legitimate thing to want, and a course can honor it on purpose. — TZ

**3. AI drafts for discretionary work** — *Mahinpei, Dean, Fong, Liu & Horta Ribeiro, "[AI Assistance for Discretionary Work: Increasing Feedback Provision in Higher Education](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03095)," arXiv preprint, June 2026.*

*Evidence: randomized field experiment in one 300-level machine learning course (11 TAs, 88 students), plus interviews; preprint.*

Submissions were randomly assigned to come with an AI-generated feedback draft or without one. The TAs could use, edit, or ignore the drafts. With drafts available, TAs gave personalized feedback 10.8 percentage points more often (SE = 1.1). The feedback was also longer, with no drop in how useful students rated it. The authors read the drafts as "editable scaffolds that lower barriers to initiating feedback," increasing work that might otherwise go undone while keeping humans in control of the final result.

> *Editor's note:* This is a good example of a deliberate design choice. The question was not "can AI give feedback as well as a TA?" It was "we want more students to get feedback from their TA, so how can AI help that happen?" — TZ

## Institutional Movements

**1. Faculty ask for a seat at the table: Duke.** At the October 1 Arts & Sciences Council meeting, Robin Kirk (cultural anthropology) presented a resolution saying that, as part of faculty governance, "faculty should be at the table when these enterprise decisions are made" about AI ([Duke Chronicle, Oct 2](https://dukechronicle.com/article/duke-university-arts-and-sciences-council-ai-takes-center-stage-with-proposed-resolution-on-faculty-governance-bluebot-20261002)). It followed faculty criticism of the decision to give students tools such as Microsoft Copilot and ChatGPT. Kirk said the resolution would not give faculty a yes-or-no vote. Statistician Alexander Fisher asked whether it could be used to block AI contracts. A vote is expected at the next meeting or in December. At the same meeting, the university's IT office presented BlueBot, a campus information chatbot trained only on Duke data, whose stated role is to "guide, not decide."

- *Worth noting:* A council member recalled that the body once had a technology committee during the rise of computing and the internet. No one present knew what had happened to it.
- *Open question:* A seat at the table without a vote is a voice, not a veto. Is that the right balance for enterprise purchases that shape every classroom?

**2. Students petition about faculty AI use: Eastern Illinois.** The Student Senate unanimously passed a resolution, backed by a 512-signature petition, asking the president and provost for a policy on "what professors can and cannot use AI for" ([Daily Eastern News, Oct 11](https://www.dailyeasternnews.com/2026/10/11/student-senate-asks-university-to-regulate-use-of-ai-by-faculty/)). One student argued that department-by-department rules are "not equitable." In a campuswide email, the president said a forthcoming policy would cover data privacy only and "intentionally does not attempt to regulate curriculum." He asked departments to write their own AI guidelines, which he expects to vary by discipline, as academic freedom allows.

- *Worth noting:* This reverses the usual direction of AI policy. Students want a say in how *instructors* use AI, not just the other way around.
- *Open question:* Discipline-by-discipline variation protects academic freedom, but students experience it as inconsistency. How should an institution explain why choices differ, so that variation reads as deliberate rather than arbitrary?

**3. A public voice on AI: the Edinburgh Data and AI Exchange.** The University of Edinburgh's School of Social and Political Science published *Governing the Future* on **June 10, 2026**. It reports on a day-long deliberative assembly of 99 participants from government, the NHS, finance, academia, civil society, and the public ([Edinburgh](https://www.sps.ed.ac.uk/news-events/news/citizens-assembly-public-voice-ai-report); [report](https://www.ed.ac.uk/ai/data-and-ai-exchange)). The recommendations include a standing citizens' assembly on AI and society, a statutory public-benefit test before private firms can access NHS Scotland data, and clearer legal accountability when AI informs a clinical decision that causes harm. The organizer, Oliver Escobar, said AI is reshaping public life "at a pace that has outrun meaningful public input." The report cites a 2025 Ada Lovelace Institute survey in which 60% of UK adults said they had no meaningful input on government AI decisions.

> *Editor's note:* Universities are small societies, and these three items are the same question at three scales: an enterprise decision, a classroom, a country. In each case, the request is not "stop AI." It is "let us help choose." — TZ

## Most Discussed

**Whose values, chosen how?** A *New York Times* report on **September 29** described how Anthropic consulted religious and philosophical leaders as it shaped the moral character of its Claude models ([NYT](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/29/us/anthropic-claude-morals-ai.html), paywalled). Commentary has been split. Some found the outreach thoughtful, and others objected to treating a model as a potential moral agent at all ([National Review](https://www.nationalreview.com/corner/ai-claude-can-never-be-a-person/amp/); [APS coverage](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/anthropic-wants-claude-to-be-moral-is-religion-really-the-answer.html)). Whatever one thinks of the effort, it shows that a model's values are *chosen*, and that the choosing is currently done mostly by the companies that build them. (For an example of what happens when objectives conflict and no one has said which comes first, see [Helpful to Whom?](../2026-10-15-helpful-to-whom/).)

- *Open question:* Universities run on shared governance. When a campus licenses a model, it also adopts the values someone else chose for it. Should campuses ask vendors to explain those choices, the way they ask about data privacy?

**Question for discussion: capability or preference?** Many campus AI debates are framed as capability questions: is AI good enough to grade, tutor, advise, or write? But capability changes every few months. A policy that rests on "AI can't do X yet" goes out of date when it can. A policy that rests on "we want a person to do X, for these reasons" holds until our reasons change, and then we can revisit it deliberately.

## Interesting Ideas & Repos

**1. "Guide, not decide."** Duke's BlueBot team uses this four-word design principle, and it is a clear way to state the role a tool is meant to play ([Duke Chronicle](https://dukechronicle.com/article/duke-university-arts-and-sciences-council-ai-takes-center-stage-with-proposed-resolution-on-faculty-governance-bluebot-20261002)).

**2. Editable drafts instead of automated outputs.** The feedback experiment above gave TAs drafts they could use, edit, or ignore, instead of sending AI feedback straight to students ([Mahinpei et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03095)). The design keeps the human role, and the human accountability, intact.

**3. Standing deliberative bodies.** Edinburgh's recommendation of a *standing* citizens' assembly, rather than a one-off consultation, fits a technology that keeps changing ([Edinburgh](https://www.sps.ed.ac.uk/news-events/news/citizens-assembly-public-voice-ai-report)). The campus version might be a standing faculty–student AI committee, like the technology committee Duke's council once had.

**4. Two readings on choice.**

- **Joseph Weizenbaum, *Computer Power and Human Reason* (1976).** The creator of ELIZA, the first chatbot, argued that some tasks should not be handed to computers at all. In a [1976 reply to John McCarthy](https://www.saildart.org/WEIZEN.ANS[PUB,JMC]) he put it in capitals, "THERE ARE TASKS THAT COMPUTERS SHOULD NOT BE PROGRAMMED TO DO," and called it "genuinely a main point of the book." His best-known line still rests partly on ability: "Since we do not now have any ways of making computers wise, we ought not now to give computers tasks that demand wisdom." Jacob Silverman's [2024 essay in *The New Republic*](https://newrepublic.com/article/181189/inventor-chatbot-tried-warn-us-ai-joseph-weizenbaum-computer-power-human-reason) is a good introduction.
- **Sheila Jasanoff, [*The Ethics of Invention: Technology and the Human Future*](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/ethics-invention-technology-and-human-future) (W. W. Norton, 2016).** Jasanoff moves the argument from individual refusal to collective choice. She rejects the idea that technology is neutral or that its path is fixed. She shows how societies quietly hand power to technological systems and argues for open, democratic debate about both the risks and the promise of new technologies, guided by a shared picture of the world we want to live in.

## What Changed

- **Restraint as a choice, continued.** The October 14 issue noted the University of Chicago's social sciences division banning AI from its core courses while its law school builds AI into electives. Eastern Illinois now shows the student side of the same issue: department-by-department choices protect academic freedom, but students feel them as inconsistency.
- **Augmentation evidence, from the instructor's side.** Earlier issues collected studies on AI tutors and student learning. The TA-feedback experiment adds evidence on the instructor's side: AI can help people do work they value without taking over that work.

## From My Desk

I realized recently that I had been framing AI governance the wrong way. A lot of our discussion, mine included, goes into working out whether humans are better than AI at certain things: grading, tutoring, writing, even moral reasoning. Each side brings evidence, and the answer seems to settle the policy. If AI does it better, hand it over; if not, keep it human.

But governance is not a contest. It is about human preference: how we, as people, choose to use technology in our lives on this earth. Even if a system could match or beat a person at a task, we might still choose to do it ourselves. We might value the relationship it builds, the learning it produces, or the accountability it keeps with a person. The reverse holds too. We may happily hand a task to a machine that does it no better than we do, because we would rather spend our time on something else. Both are legitimate choices. Neither is settled by a benchmark.

The same holds inside our own teaching. Keeping people in the loop is a *preference*, not a logical necessity. If throughput were the only goal, much of what we train students to do could be handed off. We keep the human in because we value having a person exercise judgment, authorship, and accountability. Once I admit that, the task changes. It stops being "prove the human is necessary" and becomes "decide which human role we want to preserve, design for it, and train people to be good at it." ForkSCAPE, a multiverse-analysis tool I've been building, is one attempt at the training side. Instead of collapsing a dataset to a single defensible answer, it surfaces the many defensible analytic paths and leaves the choice among them to a person, so the human keeps practicing the part that is genuinely judgment.

Two things in this issue make me more confident about this. First, the Hüholt and Szech experiments show that "the AI is capable" is not a neutral fact we discover. It is a belief we adjust, sometimes to excuse what we already wanted to do. Second, the Duke, Eastern Illinois, and Edinburgh items show that people are already asking the right question. They are not asking whether the machine is good enough. They are asking to be part of the choice.

Weizenbaum saw part of this fifty years ago when he argued that some tasks should not be given to computers. Jasanoff takes it further: deciding what a technology is *for* is a democratic act, and we give that act away when we treat the technology's path as inevitable. For universities, this means that when a department decides how students may use AI in a course, the deciding question is not whether AI writes better than a first-year student. It is what we want that course to develop in the student, which human role we want to preserve, and whether the curriculum trains that role or merely certifies familiarity with the tools. Different departments will reasonably answer differently, because they value different things. That is not inconsistency. It is governance working as it should, provided we explain our choices and keep the people affected in the conversation. — TZ

## Try This Week

### Rewrite one capability question as a preference question

Pick one AI decision in front of your course, department, or committee. If it is framed as "Can AI do X as well as we can?", rewrite it as "Do we want AI to do X here, and why?" Write down the reasons. They are what your policy should rest on, and they will last longer than any capability benchmark.

### Ask the people affected

Before your next AI-related course decision, ask your students a short question: "Which parts of this course do you want a person to do, even if AI could?" Their answers may surprise you. As the Eastern Illinois petition shows, students have views on how *instructors* use AI too.
